# Barnaby's blog voice

This guide describes the feel of Barnaby's writing: its rhythm, cadence, humour and level of explanation. It can help with anything from editing a sentence to turning a handful of ideas into a complete post. How much Barnaby writes, edits or delegates will vary with the task. His current direction takes precedence.

Read [existing posts](../src/posts/) for examples, including their headings and captions. Those provide an evolving reference without this document needing an update after every article. Read for the way the prose moves and the choices that fit each subject, rather than collecting phrases to reuse.

The observations here are tendencies, with room for judgment. There is no requirement to include a joke, flourish, anecdote, aside or particular structure. A straightforward passage can sound entirely like Barnaby. Trying to demonstrate every characteristic at once will usually make the writing feel less natural.

## The overall feel

This is a personal blog: somewhere for Barnaby's experiences, experiments and opinions to have his name attached. The writing is comfortable territory for engineers and interested technical leaders, without being organised around a buyer persona by default.

It sounds like someone talking through something they have been working on with another person who understands software. Familiar words, contractions and specific observations give it an easy, conversational feel. There is little ceremony around introducing a subject or explaining why the reader should care.

Opinions tend to come with the experience that produced them. Barnaby can be confident, frustrated, enthusiastic or unsure, and can reconsider an earlier view. Once the context is clear, the prose does not need to keep announcing “for me” or “in my experience”. Personal scope allows a clear opinion without claiming to settle the subject for everyone.

## Rhythm and cadence

The default is concise, connected prose with varied sentence lengths. A longer explanation can be followed by a short reaction. Fragments sometimes sound exactly right after a technical passage; a whole article made of emphatic fragments would feel affected.

The writing often gets its energy from a concrete sequence: what Barnaby was trying, where it became awkward, what he did about it. Details carry the explanation. A sentence does not need extra qualifications or a winding introduction to make it conversational.

Paragraphs follow the thought. Some are brief, others need several sentences. There is no fixed paragraph size, sentence pattern or ratio of short to long sentences. Reading aloud is useful for hearing whether a passage flows or has acquired a rehearsed rhythm.

Parentheses can carry a small aside or grumble. They work best when the main thought remains easy to follow. A lengthy aside can often become a shorter phrase, a separate sentence or something to leave out. Punctuation serves the passage; ellipses and other conversational touches do not need to become recurring signals of personality.

Australian English is the default, including spellings such as “parallelise”, “colour” and “behaviour”. Its influence is mostly a relaxed register and natural phrasing. Being Australian does not call for inserting “mate” or other regional slang. Recognisable catchphrases, character references and verbal tics should not become a greeting or signature that follows the author from subject to subject.

## Humour and personality

Understated humour is comfortable here. An occasional grand, tongue-in-cheek flourish can be fun, especially when aimed at Barnaby's own confidence or habits. Its effect comes partly from the plainer prose around it.

The humour responds to what is being discussed. A particular word, pop-culture reference or meme may fit one moment beautifully and have no place in the next post. There is no quota for jokes or flourishes. Serious or practical passages can stay that way, and a joke rarely benefits from being explained or followed by another one.

Frustration can be candid without becoming a performance of outrage. Swearing, emoji, silly footnotes and parenthetical grumbles are available when they fit, used sparingly and tastefully. Barnaby does not regard “hell” as swearing in Australian usage. Permission to use these devices is not a suggestion to add them.

Typos and accidental repetition can be corrected without sanding off personality. The individual observation, choice of detail and way a thought unfolds are what make the writing personal. Deliberate mistakes, filler and forced slang do not help.

## Examples of the feel

These excerpts illustrate choices that worked in their original context. The useful reference is the relationship between the sentences, the detail and the tone. Neither the wording nor the device needs to recur in another post.

**An occasional flourish aimed at himself:**

> I had built one, so with a haughty confidence I felt qualified to define the whole category.

The confidence is knowingly overplayed for a moment. Most surrounding prose is much plainer.

**A short reaction after a more technical thought:**

> But I was picturing Kanbans, Jiras, Agiles, and Agents. Big team stuff.

The fragment sounds like an observation made in conversation. This is one possible rhythm, rather than a pattern for every paragraph.

**Frustration carried by concrete experience:**

> I had notes for each ticket to remember where it was up to. I even had a Trello board at one point, but I was managing all of it manually, which was a huge PIA and exhausting.

The account makes the frustration understandable without an invented dramatic incident or an extra claim about productivity.

**Technical detail connected to a reason:**

> I needed to know what an agent was up to, including its token usage and tool calls. Those were things that mattered to me, so I built around them.

There is enough information to explain the choice, without turning the passage into a feature tour.

**A joke that belongs to a particular moment:**

> So simple, slap some agents into a Kanban, you got yourself a software factory bucko.

The meme and the following “Or not” make sense in that article. “Bucko”, that construction and the change of direction are not signature elements to carry into unrelated writing. The same applies to playful headings and the Flintstones caption: the reference fits the subject at hand.

## Explanation, shape and length

The amount of explanation follows what the reader needs to understand the thought. References such as Ralph loops, harnesses or the SDLC can appear without a detour when the context is sufficient. An unfamiliar concept may still deserve an explanation if the story depends on it.

Technical detail is most useful when it explains a decision, frustration or result. An account of building something does not automatically need a walkthrough of the whole system. Supporting examples can sometimes do their work in a sentence or two.

There is no prescribed story arc. A post might tell a story, explore an idea, explain a technique or work through a question. When an opinion changes, the reason for that change matters. When an approach stops working, preserving where it had worked gives the account more substance.

Repetition made an early draft of the software-factory post feel long: it described the workflow, then revisited the same perspective under another heading. The broader preference is to keep the thought moving. Headings mark useful turns, and paragraphs or lists take the shape the material needs. There is no target number of sections or fixed word count.

An ending can simply let the thought land. A recap, grand lesson or engagement question often adds little once that has happened. A commercial paragraph can belong in a post when wanted, without becoming the default ending of personal writing.

Visuals and captions can participate in the same tone. A screenshot can carry practical detail; a caption or small interaction can carry a joke. The elaborate factory title and other treatments in the first post are particular creative choices, with no obligation to repeat that level of decoration elsewhere.

## What tends to make it sound like an LLM

Generic industry scene-setting, unsupported declarations about the future and universal advice tend to move the writing away from Barnaby's actual thought. So do staged revelations and TED-talk rhetoric, particularly “This is an X, not a Y” constructions.

Other signs are repeated slogan-like summaries, artificially balanced contrasts, neat three-part lists added for rhythm, and a dramatic standalone sentence at every turn. Any individual device can occur naturally. The problem is the conspicuous pattern or the sense that the prose has been arranged to sound impressive.

Overdoing the voice is another version of the same problem: obligatory slang, a stock aside, a familiar joke or a theatrical flourish inserted because the guide mentioned it. Follow the subject and the thought. The examples are there to help hear the cadence, not supply a set of mannerisms.

Facts still need a basis. A fluent passage should not invent Barnaby's experiences, dialogue, results or other people's views to make the story work. Missing information can remain a question or a placeholder while the surrounding writing develops.
